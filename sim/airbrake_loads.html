Air Brake Load Lab

How hard does the servo have to work to push the air brake out, and when does it lose? Change the rocket, the leaves, the servo and the flight condition on the left. Everything recomputes as you type.

Tilt is not the enemy in flight

After burnout the rocket and the leaves fall together, so gravity pulls no leaf in or out. A rocket 30° off vertical loads its leaves the same as a vertical one at the same airspeed.

Airspeed is

Load grows with airspeed squared. Twice the speed means four times the torque. The deploy moment right after burnout is the hardest point of the flight.

Angle of attack is a small extra

Angle of attack is the angle between the rocket and the air it flies through (gusts). A stable rocket keeps it to a few degrees. It shifts load between leaves; it does not depend on tilt.

Fastest airspeed the brake can still open at

For each angle of attack, the airspeed at which peak servo torque equals what the servo can supply. Deploy below this line and the leaves go out; above it the servo stalls.

Coast flight

A 2D coast from burnout to apogee using the rocket inputs. The servo is commanded open at the deploy time; if the load gets close to its limit it slows down, and at the limit it stalls. Angle of attack comes from the gust speed (atan of gust over airspeed).

What the model includes

Frame of reference. Loads are worked out in the rocket's own frame. In flight the only felt acceleration is from aerodynamic forces (drag pushes everything toward the nose, normal force pushes sideways). Gravity cancels because the rocket is in free fall. On the bench the rocket is held, so gravity is felt in full and split by the tilt angle.

Sliding leaves (the design in this repo). Each exposed leaf face makes drag = Cd × q × width × extension, with q = ½ρV². That force is across the sliding direction, so it only resists the servo through friction (μ). With drawer binding on, the drag acting on the overhanging part cocks the leaf in its tray, like a heavy drawer, and adds friction that grows with extension. Crossflow pressure on the leaf tip edge pushes the windward leaf in and pulls the leeward one out, using measured-shape cylinder pressures. Servo torque comes from the offset slider-crank solved the same way as airbrake_stl.py: torque = force × (leaf travel per radian of crank). At full extension the linkage is at dead centre, so the torque goes to zero there.

Hinged flaps. Flat-plate normal force with a low-aspect-ratio fit, Cn = (π·AR/2)·sin i·cos i + 1.17·sin² i, centre of pressure moving from 25% to 50% chord. Hinge at the front: the air pushes the flap shut, the servo must push it open. Hinge at the back: the flap scoops air and is blown open, so the servo must hold it back. Angle of attack changes each flap's incidence by α·cos(angle to the windward side).

Servo and power. Available torque = stall torque × min(1, boost converter current ÷ servo stall current). A servo pulling more current than the converter can give sags the voltage instead of making torque. Under load the servo slows along a straight torque-speed line.

Left out. Leaf bending, tray deflection, vibration, Mach effects (fine below about 0.3 Mach, 100 m/s), the boundary layer shielding part of the leaf (which makes the real drag a bit lower), and roll. Defaults are guesses for a 2.6 in rocket aiming at 800 ft; replace them with your numbers, especially μ, leaf mass and the servo figures. The best check is a measured tunnel or car-window test logged in testing/airbrake_tunnel_log.xlsx.
